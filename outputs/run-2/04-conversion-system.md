### Sales Path

The path follows the value ladder from 02-offer-architecture.md. The video only sells the template. Each rung is the next useful step from the one before, and each rung extends the same revenue system.

1. **Video (free content).** Shows one idea going through the Revenue Chain with a visible timer. The proof is the specific output, the evidence labels and the contract sentence appearing in four places. Single CTA: the template.
2. **Lead magnet (template, email captured).** The exact seven files shown on screen: four agent files, the brief, the runbook and the one-page setup guide. The reader runs the chain on their own idea. Revenue tie: they leave with their own connected assets, which is the thing the paid rungs improve, not replace.
3. **Emails 1 to 3 (activation).** Get the reader to a completed first run and a self-check. Qualification signals to watch, treat as hypotheses to validate: replies describing a real deliverable service; replies saying output was generic and asking for niche tuning; replies stuck on setup. Each signal points to a different next step (workshop, a fix to the runbook, a simpler setup guide).
4. **Email 4 (frame).** The systems-versus-tools argument and a first mention of the workshop, with no signup required to read the outline.
5. **Email 5 (workshop).** A live Revenue Agent build: one idea in, the full asset pack out, four agents customized to the niche, with feedback. HYPOTHESIS price band to test with the audience before publishing: low-to-mid hundreds of USD. No price appears in the video or the emails until it is tested.
6. **Premium service (done-with-you AI revenue systems build).** A custom agent team for a solo expert's offer, content and follow-up, plus review of the first live outputs. HYPOTHESIS: high-ticket, scoped per client, capacity-limited. Offered by reply only, and not sold on proof until there are two or three real builds with permission. Until then, describe it as a scoped engagement and state plainly that it is not a results guarantee.

What moves someone up a rung: demonstrated doing (they ran the chain), a named need (generic output, no feedback loop, no time), and a clear fit. What does not: urgency, countdowns, claims about income.

Measure from day one (hypotheses to validate, no benchmarks assumed): video to download rate, download to first-run reply rate, replies by type (setup stuck, generic output, ready for help), workshop page visits from Email 4 and Email 5. Use the reply types to decide whether to fix the template, change the workshop outline, or open the premium service.

### Friction Check

Items that would make the conversion path feel confusing or forced, with the fix:

1. **The offer sentence is long and written in the creator's "I help" voice.** Read aloud in the hook, in the lead magnet promise and again in Email 1, it can feel repetitive. This is the contract, so it cannot be trimmed. Mitigation: always introduce it with a plain label ("this is the promise the whole thing is built around") and let the on-screen outlining show why it repeats.
2. **"Single working session" is not yet measured.** The sentence promises it, but the offer architecture says no minute figure until the timer shows one. Keep every email and the CTA free of minutes. If the timed run is far longer than a session, change the hypothesis, not the video.
3. **Template must match the screen file for file.** If the folder includes anything not shown (a fifth agent, extra files, a different runbook), trust breaks. Verify the folder against the 12:30 side-by-side shot before publishing: exactly seven files (four agents, blank brief, runbook, one-page setup guide).
4. **Runbook may need cleanup before it is given away.** It is the coordinator's operating document. Confirm it contains nothing internal (client names, private notes).
5. **Setup is the biggest drop-off risk.** The video skips installation by design, and the audience fears the terminal. The one-page setup guide must be written for a non-developer and must exist before the template ships. Email 2 offers reply-for-help. If setup replies pile up, improve the setup guide rather than putting installation back in the video.
6. **Workshop and premium details do not exist yet.** Email 4 and Email 5 contain placeholders (title, date, link, length). Do not send them until the page is live. If the workshop is not scheduled, change Email 5 to a waitlist and say so honestly. Do not fake a date.
7. **Price.** No price appears anywhere. Publishing one before testing it is a forced-sale signal and may be wrong.
8. **Premium service offered without proof.** Until two or three real builds exist, the "build" reply option should be described as a scoped conversation, and no result or case study may be implied.
9. **Second CTA in the video.** The workshop is mentioned once, conditionally. If it is repeated in the close or on a second lower-third, the video becomes a pitch. Keep one link on screen.
10. **Read-only researcher.** The market-signal-researcher cannot write files, so the main session must save its brief. If this is not explained in the setup guide's coordinator note, first-time users will think the first step failed.
11. **Claims drift.** Scan every page and email for the words "guarantee", "leads", "revenue" and "income" and keep them only in the context of "this does not promise". No testimonials, screenshots or numbers unless the creator has real, showable cases.
12. **Demo idea.** The hook's example idea is a placeholder. If the chain's output for the final demo idea reads generic, the whole path (video, magnet, emails) loses credibility, so change the idea and rerun before filming.
13. **List consent and basics.** The opt-in page needs a clear statement that the five-email follow-up is coming, an unsubscribe link, and a real sender address. The CTA already says a short note will follow; keep it consistent with the emails.
14. **Email length and link count.** Each email has one link or one reply action. Do not add a second link to the template re-download in Emails 2 to 5; point to Email 1 instead.
