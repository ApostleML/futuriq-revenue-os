### One-Sentence Offer

I help first-time engineering managers hold their first underperformance conversation with a former peer within a week, without avoiding it or souring the relationship, by building a rehearsed, reviewed conversation runbook.

### Positioning

For a newly promoted engineering manager whose former teammate now reports to them and is underperforming, this is the one conversation runbook you build, rehearse and get reviewed before the meeting, so you stop putting it off. It is not a "first 90 days" course and not a leadership philosophy. It covers one painful moment, handled like an engineering problem: define the failure, write the plan, test it, then ship.

Narrowing [DECISION]: the market brief calls "first 90 days" crowded (books, GitLab and LeadDev lists, paid e-books, free vendor PDFs) [MARKET]. I picked one scenario from its candidate list: the first underperformance conversation with a former peer. It combines two of the brief's candidates (managing a former peer, first underperformance conversation) because in practice they are one moment: the person you used to code beside is missing the bar, and you have to say so. Other scenarios (first 1:1 template, deciding what to stop coding) become later videos and later program modules. Do not add them to this video.

Chosen buyer for this video: the individual manager [DECISION]. Why:
- The video speaks to someone in a specific situation. The person living that situation is the one who feels the urgency and clicks. [MARKET, INFERENCE]
- An employer sale needs a different motion (L&D contact, business case, procurement, group pricing) and a different message (retention, ramp time). The brief says not to mix buyers in one video, and the creator has no proof to support an employer pitch yet. [MARKET]
- The risk with self-pay is real: the market brief doubts a first-time manager pays a premium to an unproven coach [MARKET, INFERENCE]. So the ladder below starts with low-risk, low-price steps and treats employer reimbursement as a later, separate path, not as this video's buyer.

### Core Offer

Working name [PLACEHOLDER: coach to name it; suggested working label "Former-Peer Conversation Runbook"].

What the paid product is: a structured prep-and-review of one real upcoming conversation, then a short follow-through plan. The viewer arrives with an actual situation (anonymized) and leaves with:
1. A written one-page runbook for the conversation: the observed facts, the gap against a stated expectation, the opening lines, the likely pushbacks and replies, the support you will offer, and the follow-up date.
2. A rehearsal with the coach playing the report, with notes on where the script breaks.
3. A follow-up plan for the next two to four weeks (check-in cadence, what "improved" looks like in observable terms, when to escalate to your own manager or HR).

Measurable outcome (a process outcome, not a performance promise):
- The conversation is held within 7 days of the review session.
- Written expectations exist and a follow-up date is set.
- What it does not promise: that the person improves, stays, or stays friendly. The coach cannot control that and should not claim it.

Who it is for: engineers in their first management role (or about to start), roughly the first 6 to 12 months, with a direct report who used to be a peer and is missing the bar, and who is currently avoiding the conversation. [DECISION: the 6 to 12 month window is a working assumption; the market brief only supports "acute during the first 3 to 6 months" and that is itself weak evidence.]

Painful problem: the manager keeps putting off the conversation (or quietly does the person's work), the gap grows, the team notices, and the manager's own boss may notice too. Fears named in the market brief that this touches: hurting team trust, being quietly judged by their own manager, looking incompetent. [MARKET, INFERENCE]

Why now:
- The trigger is a real event, and the problem gets heavier each week the conversation is delayed. That cost of delay is [INFERENCE]. The coach should not cite any statistic for it unless they have a real one.
- Public studies describe the engineer-to-manager transition as a recognized struggle and say supervisors underestimate it [MARKET, PUBLIC, small and older samples]. It is safe to say "this is a known hard part". It is not safe to say "most new managers fail".

Why this is not generic: it is one scenario, one deliverable, one deadline.

Prices: all [HYPOTHESIS], to be tested, not claimed as market rates. Set them in the value ladder below.

### Unique Mechanism

This is a coaching program, not an AI product, so I am not forcing an AI angle [DECISION per request]. The mechanism that makes the promise believable is a "debug the conversation" loop that engineers already understand, not a leadership philosophy.

The loop, working name "Conversation Runbook" [PLACEHOLDER: coach to confirm or rename; the exact steps below must be what the coach genuinely teaches]:

1. Reproduce: write down only observable facts about the work (dates, deliverables, review comments). No labels like "unmotivated". The observable-behavior-and-impact structure here is the widely taught Situation-Behavior-Impact feedback model (credited to the Center for Creative Leadership). The video should name it as a public framework, not as the coach's invention. [PUBLIC knowledge, confirm the attribution before filming]
2. Define expected behavior: state the bar in one sentence that would be the same for any report, which separates the standard from the friendship.
3. Write the opening: the first 30 seconds are scripted word for word, because that is where managers flinch. The former-peer relationship gets named out loud once, on purpose.
4. Test the failure cases: list the 3 most likely replies ("you did this too", "nobody told me", silence) and write a response to each.
5. Rehearse: say it out loud, ideally with a second person playing the report. This is the part a template cannot do, and it is what the paid product sells.
6. Ship and monitor: hold the conversation, send a short written recap, set the follow-up date, check against the observable facts, not mood.

Why a skeptical engineer might believe it: it is transparent and falsifiable (they can run it on their own situation tonight), it uses debug and runbook vocabulary rather than HR language, and the free version really works as a template. The paid product is the rehearsal and review of their actual case. That is a clean line between free and paid. [DECISION]

Honesty constraint: steps 1 to 6 are a reasonable structure built on public feedback practice. Until the coach supplies evidence that the process worked for real people, it must be presented as "the process I use" and never as "proven to" anything. See Blockers.

Optional AI use, not part of the core claim: the viewer could use an LLM as a practice partner for rehearsal. If the coach wants it, mention it as a tip, with a privacy warning (do not paste real names or performance details into a public tool). It is not the mechanism and should not appear in the one-sentence offer.

### Proof Requirements

What the audience needs to trust it, split by what can honestly be shown today versus what the coach must supply.

Can be shown without any credentials:
- The runbook itself on screen, filled in, using a clearly labeled fictional scenario ("hypothetical, not a client"). Show the actual opening 30 seconds as text.
- A live demonstration: the coach playing both manager and report in a short role-play, labeled as a demonstration. Shows judgment and craft directly.
- Public sources cited on screen with links, claims limited to what they support: the Purdue interview study (16 new engineering managers) and the BYU study (220 managers) showing the transition is recognized as hard. Say they are small samples. [MARKET, PUBLIC]
- The Situation-Behavior-Impact model credited to its source, and the video stating where the coach's own method differs from it.
- A plain statement of limits: what the runbook cannot do (it cannot make someone improve; it does not replace HR when a case involves legal or protected-class issues).

Must come from the coach (blockers, see below) before the video can carry authority claims:
- Who the coach is and why they are qualified, stated truthfully.
- Any real client work, shown only with permission and anonymized.
- Any testimonials, quoted with consent and verbatim.

If the coach has none of these yet, the video should say so honestly and lean on "here is the process, run it yourself, judge it", and the paid step should be positioned as a pilot at a lower price with a satisfaction policy. Do not imply otherwise. [DECISION]

### Objection Map

1. "I can get this from a book." The video should concede it: the books are good (the market brief lists Watkins, Stanier and Drasner among those commonly recommended) [MARKET, PUBLIC]. Books cover principles; none of them rehearse your specific conversation with your specific person. Show the gap on screen: a general principle versus the exact opening line for this case.
2. "Who are you? Why should I trust you?" Weakest point; credibility scored 1 of 5 [MARKET]. Address it in the first minute: state the coach's real background [PLACEHOLDER], what the coach has and has not done, and invite the viewer to test the free runbook before spending anything. Never fake authority.
3. "A script will sound robotic to my engineers." The runbook scripts only the first 30 seconds and the three likely pushbacks, then you go off-script. Demonstrate it in the role-play. Engineers tend to distrust fluff, so show the structure, not slogans. [INFERENCE]
4. "My situation is different (remote, a senior engineer, a friend, a cultural issue)." Say honestly which cases the runbook fits and which need HR, legal or a different approach. Narrow scope is part of the credibility.
5. "Is this worth paying for when I can ask my own manager or a mentor?" Concede that a good mentor is great. The paid session is for people who don't have one available on this topic or want an independent rehearsal partner outside the company chain. Do not disparage managers.
6. "I'm not sure I even want to be a manager." The market brief notes some hope to return to IC [MARKET, INFERENCE]. The video can say that avoiding the conversation is a data point either way, and that handling it is not a commitment to management forever. Do not push anyone to stay.
7. "Privacy: I can't share details about my report." Offer an anonymization format (no names, no company, generic role) in the template and session intake. [DECISION]
8. "It's too expensive for something I might do wrong anyway." Keep the entry price low, state what is and isn't promised (the conversation happens with a written plan; outcomes aren't guaranteed), and offer a refund or no-fit policy [PLACEHOLDER].
9. "My company should pay." Fair. Provide a one-paragraph reimbursement request they can send. Do not turn this into an employer sales pitch in this video. [DECISION]

### Summary for later agents

Quote the One-Sentence Offer above word for word. The strongest honest angle is a single painful scenario plus a free usable template, with the paid step being the rehearsal and review of the viewer's own case. The weakest link is credibility; without the coach's real details the script should rely on the process demonstrated and public sources, and should not use authority language.
