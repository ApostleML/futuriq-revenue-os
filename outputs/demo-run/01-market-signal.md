## Market Signal Brief: Leadership coaching for first-time engineering managers (demo-run)

Evidence labels used below:
- **[BRIEF]**: stated in outputs/demo-run/business-brief.md.
- **[ASSUMPTION]**: an assumption the brief itself flags.
- **[PUBLIC]**: seen in two quick web searches. This is thin evidence. I did not see search volume, view counts, pricing or conversion data, so none of that is claimed.
- **[INFERENCE]**: my reasoning, not verified.

### What the brief gives us
- The idea is a leadership coaching program for first-time engineering managers. [BRIEF]
- The coach is unnamed. The core promise is undefined and the offer-architect owns it. No credentials or results exist to cite. [BRIEF]
- The video goal (under 15 minutes), lead magnet path and voice are all [ASSUMPTION]s.
- Constraint: no invented testimonials, results, income claims or credentials. [BRIEF]

### Demand Pattern

**Public evidence ([PUBLIC], limited)**
- Academic work treats the engineer-to-manager transition as a known problem.
  - A Purdue interview study of 16 newly transitioned engineering managers found most struggled, especially early on. [Purdue](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI30504237)
  - A BYU study of 220 engineering managers built on earlier work identifying 9 common pain points. [BYU](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/1715)
  - An ASEE paper reports that technical professionals have difficulty moving into management and that their immediate supervisors consistently underestimated the difficulty. Only the abstract was visible to me. [ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/assessing-the-training-of-technical-professionals-moving-into-management.pdf)
  - These are small, mostly older, academic samples. They show the problem is recognized. They do not show demand or willingness to pay.
- A content and product ecosystem already exists, so this is not an empty niche.
  - LeadDev has a "10 essential books for new software engineering managers" list. [LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/career-development/10-essential-books-new-software-engineering-managers)
  - GitLab's handbook has a recommended reading list for its engineering managers. [GitLab](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/devops/create/engineering-managers/books/)
  - Titles on those lists include Watkins' *The First 90 Days*, Stanier's *Become an Effective Software Engineering Manager* and Drasner's *Engineering Management for the Rest of Us*.
  - A Leadership in Tech newsletter piece, "The Myth of the First 90 Days", argues the 90-day ramp is too slow in fast-moving tech. [Leadership in Tech](https://leadershipintech.com/newsletters/1986-the-myth-of-the-first-90-days)
  - Paid first-90-days e-books exist, including one on Gumroad from a management coach aimed at engineers. [Gumroad](https://tuesdaystrong.gumroad.com/l/first90days)
  - Vendors publish free first-90-days guides, such as an Echometer PDF.
  - Reading these results as a pattern, "first 90 days" is the dominant framing and is crowded. That is [INFERENCE].
- I found no dedicated engineering-manager coaching newsletter in these results. That is a weak gap signal. It does not prove a gap.

**Inference on search behavior ([INFERENCE], unverified)**
- Likely queries: "new engineering manager mistakes", "first time engineering manager", "how to run 1:1s", "how to give feedback to engineers", "engineer to manager transition", "managing former peers", "should I go back to IC".
- The likely trigger is a promotion or an offer, so demand probably arrives in bursts tied to career events.
- Engineers likely search by situation ("my star engineer is underperforming") more than by credential ("leadership coaching").

### Audience Psychology

All of this section is [INFERENCE] unless marked. The only grounding is the public pattern that the transition is hard, that managers are often underprepared, and that supervisors underestimate the difficulty ([PUBLIC], above).

- **Audience level:** intermediate to skeptical.
  - They are technically advanced but new to people management.
  - They are suspicious of "leadership" content that reads as MBA or HR language.
  - The brief's "credible to engineers, no fluff" voice [ASSUMPTION] matches this.
- **Wants:**
  - A playbook with concrete scripts, templates and checklists.
  - To avoid looking incompetent to their team and their boss.
  - To keep technical respect while no longer being the best coder on the team.
  - Proof that they won't fail in the first 6 months.
- **Fears:**
  - Hurting their team's trust.
  - Losing technical skills.
  - Being quietly judged by their own manager.
  - Having taken the wrong career path.
- **Misunderstands:**
  - That management is "more of the same job with a bigger scope".
  - That fixing problems personally still works.
  - That a team's output still equals their own output.
  - That it is only a skills gap rather than an identity shift.
- **Secretly hopes:**
  - That a system or framework exists, like debugging, that they can learn quickly.
  - That someone will tell them it is normal to feel lost.
  - That they can go back to IC without penalty.
- **Who pays:**
  - Self-pay seems unlikely at premium price from a first-time manager with an unproven coach. This is [INFERENCE].
  - An employer's L&D budget is plausible. The brief's second-buyer idea is an [ASSUMPTION] and would need a separate sales path.

### Risk

What would make the video feel generic or unbelievable:
- **Generic content.** "5 mistakes new managers make", "be a servant leader" or "build trust" with no concrete example. The topic is crowded with books and guides [PUBLIC].
- **Unbelievable claims.** Any result, transformation, promotion or retention claim. None can be made, because the coach has no credentials or proof [BRIEF].
- **Fake authority.** Invented testimonials, a made-up client story or a borrowed "I led teams at X" voice. All banned [BRIEF].
- **Wrong register.** Corporate leadership vocabulary in front of skeptical engineers.
- **Funnel mismatch.** The video teaches a complete system, so the viewer has no reason to want the program. Or the reverse: the video is a pitch with no value.
- **Wrong buyer.** Pricing and positioning aimed at employers while the content speaks to individuals.

### Strategic Recommendation

- **Fix credibility before anything else.** The coach must supply real proof (background, experience, genuine client work), or the video should lean on demonstrated process and clearly labeled public frameworks instead of authority. Unfilled placeholders are not acceptable. Flag this as a blocker for the offer-architect.
- **Pick one narrow, painful scenario instead of "first 90 days".** The topic is crowded [PUBLIC]. Examples to test (all [INFERENCE]): managing a former peer, a first underperformance conversation, a first 1:1 template, or deciding what to stop coding. A scenario with a visible script fits the under-15-minute limit.
- **Teach one concrete tool on screen.** A template, script or decision rule they can use the same day, in an engineer-friendly format (checklist or "debug your 1:1"). Do not promise results.
- **Make the funnel logical.** The free lead magnet should be the template or checklist for that scenario. The paid program should be the part a template can't do: personalized review, practice and feedback on their actual situation. Decide with the offer-architect whether the buyer is the individual or the employer, and do not mix them in one video.
- **Validate demand before building.** Check real YouTube and Google search volume and competitor view counts for the 3 to 4 scenario hooks. I have no such data, and the scores above for searchability and clickability should be treated as provisional until then.
